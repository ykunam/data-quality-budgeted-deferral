\noindent\textit{Short communication.}

\begin{abstract}
\vspace{-4pt}
Automated decision systems must decide which records a model may settle, where scarce human review reduces loss, and whether staff can absorb review that governance rules make compulsory. We study a knowledge-based router combining a calibrated predictor, a two-rule data-quality knowledge base, a loss estimator trained on incomplete review logs, and an allocator that enforces capacity and holds mandatory cases it cannot serve. Sorting solves this allocation exactly in feasible and overloaded regimes, and in a multi-period system the backlog does not depend on how mandatory cases are prioritized. We compared learned routers (published learning-to-defer surrogates, differential triage and a proposed two-head estimator) with cost-aware heuristics under matched information, costs, rules and capacity, using four credit datasets, nine data-quality conditions, five simulated reviewer models and 20 repeated splits with corrected tests. Pooled, no learned router beat Chow's cost-risk rule; a cost-sensitive one-vs-all surrogate and the two-head estimator were the strongest learned routers and did not differ significantly. Both beat Chow's rule on all 20 splits on the dataset with about 6,000 training records ($p<0.001$) and lost with a few hundred. Quality inputs reduced cost under outcome-informative missingness on 20 of 20 splits, via estimates of automated rather than reviewer error, but this exploratory effect was not significant after Holm adjustment. In a 40-day simulation, staffing determined backlog and cohort service levels far more than routing did.
\end{abstract}
\vspace{-6pt}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Knowledge-based systems; Learning to defer; Data quality; AI governance; Human--AI collaboration; Review capacity

\section{Introduction}
AI governance links accountability to data and system controls \cite{abraham,janssen,mikalefgov,wirtz}, yet requiring human oversight does not by itself make oversight effective \cite{green}. Managers must decide which records may be automated, where review reduces expected loss, and whether staff can meet the review demand that data-quality rules make compulsory. Rejection and learning to defer (L2D) trade model error against abstention or expert error \cite{chow,zhangreject,mozannar,verma}; rejection wrappers and uncertainty quantification rank cases by confidence \cite{mena,abdar}, which does not measure review benefit; triage estimates human and model error separately \cite{raghu}; and recent work adds workload limits \cite{alves}, sparse expert data \cite{hemmer}, quality-aware cascades \cite{farinhas} and congestion \cite{congestion}. Data-management research treats completeness and validity as measurable input properties \cite{chai,iot,mohammed}, human-in-the-loop systems depend on how human effort is deployed \cite{wuhitl,tariq}, and knowledge-based credit decision support links predictions to business actions without modeling reviewer capacity \cite{onari}. Missing is an account of what happens when quality-triggered mandatory review meets fixed capacity: records that cannot be reviewed are neither automated nor resolved, and a quality signal may predict model error, reviewer error, both or neither.

An earlier version of this study reported a 0.2\% advantage of quality-aware gain over a feature-aware comparator, using a reviewer simulated from the router's own quality signals and no published baselines. We therefore ask: \textit{under what data-quality and review-capacity conditions does quality-aware deferral reduce decision cost while satisfying explicit governance rules?} Contributions: (i) an exact solution of governance-constrained allocation in both regimes, correcting the earlier overload rule, and a proof that backlog is invariant to prioritization; (ii) a matched comparison of learned routers, including published L2D and triage methods inside the same allocator, against heuristics under reviewers that do not favor any representation, defects invisible to quality checks and an external dataset; and (iii) a conditions analysis with censoring-aware managerial service measures. We claim neither a new surrogate loss nor a uniformly better router.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Governance-constrained allocation}
For record $i$ in a batch of $n$, a calibrated predictor gives $p_i$; with costs $c_{FN}=5$, $c_{FP}=1$, the automated decision is $\hat y_i=\mathbf 1[p_i\ge 1/6]$ with loss $\ell^A_i=w(y_i)\mathbf 1[\hat y_i\ne y_i]$, $w(1)=5$, $w(0)=1$. A reviewer errs with probability $e_i$, giving expected loss $\ell^H_i=w(y_i)e_i$; review costs $c_R=0.05$ and an unresolved (held) record $c_H=1.5$. Observable quality $q_i=(u_i,v_i)$ is the missing-field and out-of-range fraction, with bounds $[Q_{.01,j}-0.1t_j,\,Q_{.99,j}+0.1t_j]$, $t_j=\max(Q_{.99,j}-Q_{.01,j},1)$, from predictor-training rows. The knowledge base marks record $i$ mandatory, $m_i=\mathbf 1[u_i\ge 0.5\lor v_i\ge 0.25]$; thresholds are illustrative organizational policy. With actions $a_i,r_i,h_i\in\{0,1\}$ (automate, review, hold) and capacity $B=\lfloor bn\rfloor$, the router solves
\begin{equation}
\min\tfrac1n\textstyle\sum_i\big[a_i\ell^A_i+r_i(\ell^H_i+c_R)+h_ic_H\big]
\label{eq:obj}
\end{equation}
subject to $a_i+r_i+h_i=1$, $\sum_ir_i\le B$, $a_i\le 1-m_i$, $h_i\le m_i$ and the service-first constraint $\sum_ih_i=(|M|-B)^+$, $M=\{i:m_i=1\}$. Let $g_i=\ell^A_i-\ell^H_i-c_R$.

\textbf{Proposition 1.} \textit{If $|M|\le B$, reviewing all of $M$ plus the $B-|M|$ optional records with the largest positive $g_i$ is optimal. If $|M|>B$, every feasible route reviews exactly $B$ mandatory records and no optional record; reviewing the $B$ smallest $\ell^H_i$ is optimal. Routes do not depend on $c_H$.} Proof: in the first case no record is held, so each optional review changes (\ref{eq:obj}) by $-g_i/n$ and an exchange argument applies. In the second, $B$ unheld mandatory records must be reviewed, exhausting capacity; only $\sum_{i\in R}\ell^H_i$ depends on the reviewed set $R$, and $c_H$ enters as the constant $(|M|-B)c_H$. $\square$ The earlier version ordered overloaded mandatory records by estimated gain, which wrongly includes $\ell^A_i$ for records that cannot be automated.

\textbf{Proposition 2.} \textit{When held records carry over between periods and Proposition 1 is applied each period, holds, backlog, reviews and completions in every period are identical for any ordering of mandatory records.} Proof: by induction, equal backlog implies equal $|M_t|$, hence equal holds $(|M_t|-B)^+$. $\square$ Staffing therefore sets the backlog, approximately growing by $(\mu_t-b)^+n$ per period for mandatory rate $\mu_t$, whereas prioritization only decides who waits.

\subsection{Loss estimation}
The allocator accepts any estimates of $\ell^A_i$ and $\ell^H_i$. Our two-head estimator fits two gradient-boosted regressors on the eight inputs $z_i=(p_i,\,s_i,\,q_i,\,p_iq_i,\,\hat y_iq_i)$, where $s_i=\min(5p_i,\,1-p_i)$ is the Bayes risk of automation: an \textit{automation head} $\hat\ell^A(z)$ fitted on all labelled records, and a \textit{reviewer head} $\hat\ell^H(z)$ fitted only on records with one logged review decision, using the unbiased target $w(y_i)\mathbf 1[\text{review}\ne y_i]$. Optional records are reviewed only if $\hat g_i=\hat\ell^A-\hat\ell^H-c_R>0$; overloaded mandatory records are ordered by $\hat\ell^H$. Allocation costs $O(n\log n)$, and each decision is logged as a label-free audit tuple $(p_i,q_i,m_i,\hat\ell^A_i,\hat\ell^H_i,\text{action})$, which documents routing but is not an explanation in the XAI sense \cite{arrieta,adadi,artelt}.

\section{Experimental protocol}
Credit scoring is a standard benchmark domain \cite{lessmann}. We use German (1,000 records), Australian (690) and default-of-credit-card (30,000) data \cite{german,australian,default}, and HMEQ (5,960; natural missingness in 11 of 12 predictors) as an external dataset unused during development \cite{hmeq}. Feature-identical records are grouped and split by majority label into 45\% predictor training, 15\% calibration, 20\% gain fitting and 20\% testing over seeds 2--21. A histogram gradient-boosting classifier (120 iterations, 15 leaves) is calibrated by logistic regression; loss heads are boosted regressors (100 iterations, seven leaves). Calibration and gain partitions are expanded into clean, missing, outlier and mixed copies.

Test sets face nine conditions: clean; Family A, defects visible to quality checks (missing, outliers, mixed, and a held-out severe shift with 35--75\% missing cells); and Family B, domain-valid defects never used in fitting (outcome-informative missingness, stale records whose continuous fields are deflated by 10--45\%, inconsistent but valid category codes, and a realistic mix). Five reviewer models are simulated: constant ($e=0.10$), quality-coupled ($\min(0.4,0.05+0.35u+0.25v)$, the earlier design), feature-dependent (a hidden projection of clean features), AI-correlated (errors concentrated where the model errs), and adverse ($0.25$); only the second uses the router's signals. All learned methods train on noisy, incomplete logs: 40\% of gain-fitting records carry one sampled reviewer decision.

Comparators share the predictor, rules, costs, capacity and allocator: confidence ($-|p_i-1/6|$), Chow cost risk ($s_i$) \cite{chow}, quality only ($u_i+v_i$), the earlier single-head gain, L2D-CE with cost-sensitive weights including the review fee \cite{mozannar}, L2D-OvA \cite{verma} trained as published and routed by a cost-sensitive plug-in rule on its calibrated outputs (expected automation loss minus expected review loss minus $c_R$), differential triage \cite{raghu}, and a probability-only two-head ablation; L2D models are one-hidden-layer networks used as rejectors only. Heuristics fill capacity; learned methods review only positive deferral scores. Because splits reuse observations, intervals and $p$-values use the corrected resampled $t$-test \cite{nadeau}, with Holm adjustment \cite{holm} within each declared family: pooled comparisons of the two-head router (8 tests), per-dataset comparisons (9 tests), and Table~2 (18 tests, exploratory). A 40-day simulation (100 arrivals per day, severe shift on days 11--20, held cases carried forward) measures managerial outcomes; service levels are computed per arrival cohort, counting cases still unresolved at day 40 as unmet and estimating resolution-time quantiles by Kaplan--Meier with censoring.

\section{Results and discussion}
\textbf{Comparison.} Table~\ref{tab:main} reports cost at 20\% capacity. All rule-constrained methods reviewed 19.8\% and held 6.0\% of records with zero rule violations, so differences reflect which records were reviewed. Pooled over datasets, Chow (0.5291) and confidence (0.5295) had the lowest cost. The strongest learned routers were L2D-OvA (0.5312) and the two-head estimator (0.5355), which did not differ significantly (14 of 20 splits favored OvA; $p=0.48$); the two-head router beat L2D-CE by 1.95\% (19/20; $p=0.014$, Holm $p=0.11$) and lost to Chow by 1.2\% (5/20; Holm $p=0.70$). Chow or confidence was lowest under four of the five reviewer models and OvA under the feature-dependent one. Dataset size explains the pattern: on default data, with about 6,000 gain-fitting records, OvA and the two-head router beat Chow by 1.4\% and 1.1\% on all 20 splits (Holm $p<0.001$), whereas on German and Australian data, with 140--200 records, both lost to Chow. In a subsampling experiment on the default partition (Fig.~\ref{fig:volume}), the two-head router was 2.0\% costlier than Chow with about 200 records, approximately competitive at 1,800 ($+0.1\%$, 10/20 splits, $p=0.85$) and cheaper only at 6,000 ($-1.1\%$, 20/20). It was the most data-efficient learned router, beating OvA on all 20 splits at 200 and 600 records (by 3.0\% and 2.7\%; Holm $p<0.005$), whereas OvA was cheapest at 6,000 ($-1.4\%$ versus Chow). This is evidence of data dependence in one dataset, not an established deployment threshold.

\textbf{When quality helps (exploratory).} Table~\ref{tab:quality} adds quality inputs to one head at a time. Under outcome-informative missingness, quality inputs in the automation head reduced cost by 2.8\% on all 20 splits, whereas in the reviewer head they did nothing; HMEQ's natural missingness showed the same pattern ($+4.5\%$, 17/20, versus $+1.0\%$). The direction is consistent with incompleteness predicting the model's own error, but no cell remains significant after Holm adjustment over the table, so the mechanism is a hypothesis for confirmatory testing. Through the reviewer head, quality inputs helped only when reviewer error was constructed from quality ($+0.7\%$, 16/20), indicating that the earlier simulation favored the representation slightly. Effects for stale records and miscoded categories, which quality checks cannot see, were small and unstable. Ordering overloaded mandatory records by $\hat\ell^H$ rather than by gain lowered cost by only 0.18\% ($p=0.47$), and the two-head estimator beat a single-head estimator only directionally ($+0.6\%$ and $+0.5\%$ with 10\% and 40\% of records logged, $-0.5\%$ with complete logs; 4--6 of 10 splits, all $p>0.5$).

\textbf{Governance and managerial outcomes.} Under severe shift the rules forced 47.5\% of records into holds and raised cost by 67\% relative to an unconstrained router, which automated 54\% of policy-barred records. Stricter thresholds cut the share of truly defective records that were automated from 80\% to 4.5\% under visible shift but left 75\% automated under invisible defects, so zero violations do not establish adequate controls. Re-optimizing every method for cost ratios of 2, 5 and 10, review fees of 0--0.2 and hold penalties of 0.5--3.0 {{E5_TEXT}}; hold penalties shifted cost linearly with identical routes, as Proposition~1 predicts. In the 40-day simulation (Fig.~\ref{fig:backlog}), mandatory demand rose from 5.5\% to 70\% of arrivals during the episode. As Proposition~2 predicts, three prioritization rules produced identical backlogs, and the fluid estimate matched peaks of 500, 300 and 200 records at 20\%, 40\% and 50\% capacity. At 20\% capacity 21.7\% of mandatory cases were still unresolved at day 40 and only 36\% of each arrival cohort was resolved within two days, although 48\% of the cases that were eventually resolved met that target; the 95th-percentile resolution time was never reached. At 40\% capacity all cases were resolved and cost per arrival fell by 45\%. Prioritization only redistributed waiting: at 40\% capacity first-in-first-out cut the Kaplan--Meier 95th-percentile resolution time from 13.9 to 7.1 days and raised seven-day resolution from 76\% to 94\%, but lowered two-day resolution from 65\% to 41\%. Reviewers overturned 44--49\% of reviewed decisions, expected under a 5:1 cost ratio. For managers, the evidence supports four practices: validate any learned router against a cost-aware heuristic on local data before relying on it; staff to mandatory rather than average demand; choose the queue discipline for its service profile; and report cohort service levels, unresolved-case age and defect coverage rather than violations alone \cite{jarrahi,capability}.

\section{Limitations}
Reviewers are simulated; real reviewers may anchor on model output, tire or change under backlog pressure, so validation with review logs is the main next step. The benchmarks are old, Family B defects are stylized, and gradual drift is not modeled \cite{lu}. Thresholds and costs are illustrative rather than elicited. The quality mechanism and three of four prespecified hypotheses are inconclusive; L2D baselines are adapted rather than tuned; data-volume evidence comes from one dataset; and fairness and legal sufficiency are not assessed.

\section{Conclusion}
Exact allocation makes governance-constrained routing auditable and lets any loss estimator, including published surrogates, operate under mandatory-review rules and capacity. No learned router beat a cost-aware heuristic in pooled data; learned routers helped where review history was large. Quality signals appeared useful under informative missingness but require confirmatory testing. Under deterioration, staffing, not routing, determined service completion.
